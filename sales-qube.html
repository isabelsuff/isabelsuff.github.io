05 — B2B training

Sales Qube

A sales training app that sharpens a team's instincts through real-time, on-the-spot objection prompts — a fast way to practise handling anything a client throws at you.

Concept — unshipped, never tested

Role
Designer
Timeline
6–8 weeks
Tools

Problem Ideation Design Where it ended
01

Problem

Traditional sales training is slow, expensive, and forgettable. Companies send teams to workshops or hand them PDFs — and then a real customer throws a curveball objection and most salespeople freeze.

The structural problem is that there is no way to practise the pressure of a live sales conversation without actually being in one. And by the time you're in one, the deal is on the line. Every rep's first few dozen attempts at handling a hard objection happen in front of a real prospect, at real cost.

Two audiences, one gap. Sales managers need something that keeps their teams sharp between formal training sessions. Salespeople need to build confidence handling objections in real time, in whatever industry they happen to sell into.

Training happens on a schedule. Objections don't. The practice has to live in the gaps.

02

Ideation

The design problem was unpredictability. What makes a real objection hard isn't its difficulty — it's that you didn't see it coming. Any format that let a rep prepare (a list, a course, a deck of cards in a fixed order) would train the wrong muscle entirely.

So the interaction had to be genuinely random, fast enough to do in the two minutes before a call, and social enough that a team could use it on each other. That last requirement is what pushed it toward a physical metaphor rather than a quiz.

03

Design decisions

1

A cube, because dice are already understood

The core interaction is a spinning cube that lands on a random objection or question. Dice carry a set of meanings people already hold: the result is out of your hands, it's fast, it's a little bit of a game, and it's something you do at someone else. That last property is the valuable one — it makes the app work as a two-person drill between colleagues, not just solo study, which is how practice actually survives in a sales team.

2

Bold and professional, not playful

A deep navy and bright blue palette with heavy, confident type. This needed care: the mechanic is a game, and a game aesthetic would have made it unusable in a workplace. A rep will not pull out something that looks like a toy in front of their manager. The visual system is deliberately closer to a CRM than to a quiz app, so the playful interaction is smuggled inside credible packaging.

3

Industry selection, so the objections are yours

A generic objection is a party trick; a relevant one is practice. Users pick their industry during setup and can change it later from settings, which is what allows one product to serve reps selling completely different things without diluting the content.

4

A subscription built on scarcity of spins

Monthly at $7.49 or annual at $59.99 (billed as $5.00/month, 33% off), with a seven-day free trial. Premium unlocks unlimited cube spins, professional response examples, client-simulated objections, and fresh objections every spin. The free tier is deliberately spin-limited rather than feature-limited — a rep who hits the ceiling mid-practice feels the value of the thing they're being asked to pay for, which is more persuasive than a locked menu item.

04

Where it ended

Sales Qube did not make it to completion. Funding ran out before the project reached user testing, so none of the decisions above were ever validated against a real salesperson. I'm including it because the problem framing and the system design are work I stand behind — but the distinction matters, and I'd rather state it than let a reader assume otherwise.

Everything here is a hypothesis. None of it survived contact with a user, because it never got the chance.

The three things I would have tested first:

1. Does randomness actually feel like pressure? The entire concept rests on the cube reproducing the surprise of a live objection. It is equally possible that a random prompt on your own phone, with no prospect watching and nothing at stake, feels like nothing at all. That's the assumption the whole product stands on and I never checked it.

2. Would anyone use it on a colleague? I designed the two-person drill as the core loop, but I never observed whether reps would really quiz each other or would quietly use it alone — which would make it a very different, and much weaker, product.

3. Is a spin limit the right wall? Limiting spins is elegant on paper but risks capping people exactly when they're engaged. Worth testing against a feature-gated free tier.

What I took from it: I spent a lot of the timeline on account creation, settings, and subscription screens — the scaffolding — and comparatively little on the cube itself, which was the only part that needed proving. If I ran this again I'd build the one uncertain interaction first and put it in front of someone in week one, long before designing a pricing page for a product that might not work.
